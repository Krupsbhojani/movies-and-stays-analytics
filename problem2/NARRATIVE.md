# Problem 2: Short-Term Rental Market Monitor

## Purpose

The dashboard is built for a **short-term rental management company** deciding where to expand and how to price the properties it manages. Companies like this run listings on behalf of owners, so they face three recurring questions: which city to enter next, what nightly price to set for a given property, and what actually earns better guest ratings and more bookings. The dashboard answers all three from one database of 5,555 Airbnb listings and about 150,000 guest reviews across 12 markets: New York, Barcelona, Montreal, Sydney, Hong Kong, Istanbul, Rio de Janeiro, Porto, and four Hawaiian islands.

## Data and architecture

The data is MongoDB's `sample_airbnb` dataset (scraped in February 2019), hosted on **Azure Cosmos DB for MongoDB**. A raw Airbnb listing is one large nested document with every review embedded inside it. That works for showing a single listing page, but it's inefficient for analytics, because every query would drag all the review text along. The loader script reshapes the data into two collections:

- **`listings`** keeps one document per property, with nested `host`, `address` and `review_scores` sub-documents and the `amenities` array. Prices are converted from `Decimal128` to floats and from local currency to USD using February 2019 exchange rates. Without that step, Hong Kong (HKD) and Rio (BRL) would look five to eight times more expensive than they are.
- **`reviews`** has one document per review, with `listing_id`, `market` and `date` copied in, so time-series questions can be answered without unwinding the listings.

Indexes cover the fields the dashboard filters on (market, room type, price, superhost status, review date). The Streamlit app runs locally and connects to the Azure cluster with a connection string kept in `.env`. The dashboard has two pages. **Board** is a landscape layout that fits every key card, including the 3D density map, on one 16:9 screen, so a manager can take in the whole market at a glance or put it on a meeting-room display. **Detailed** is the same analysis as a scrolling page, adding the market pulse tiles and a table of individual listings for analysts. Both use a filter bar, ring gauges for the headline KPIs, and then one card per question. Heavy aggregation (monthly review counts across 150k reviews, the amenity unwind) runs inside the database with aggregation pipelines. The listing-level data is pulled once with a projection and cached for 10 minutes.

## What the dashboard shows and the decisions it supports

**1. Market comparison: where to expand.** Median price alone is misleading. Istanbul and Rio de Janeiro look attractive on paper, with many listings and low prices, but only about 41% of their listings received a review in the past year, compared with 78% to 88% in Porto and the Hawaiian islands. Many of their listings are inactive, so supply is overstated. The **Opportunity map** card (price against active share, with quadrants for busy/quiet and cheap/premium) makes this visible straight away, and the **Market pulse** tiles show each market's review growth from 2016 to 2018. **Decision:** focus expansion on markets that combine a high active share with mid-range prices (Porto, Barcelona, New York) rather than markets with lots of listings but weak activity.

**2. Pricing drivers: how to price a property.** Entire homes earn about twice what private rooms do in every market. The **Price ladder** card shows that price rises roughly in line with guest capacity up to 8 guests ($51 for 1 guest, $96 for 4, about $250 for 8) and then flattens, so converting space to sleep more than 8 doesn't pay. The **Amenity premium** card compares each listing to the median for its own market and room type, so location doesn't distort the result. On that basis, an indoor fireplace (+42%), waterfront access (+32%), a hot tub (+28%), a gym (+25%) and a pool (+22%) carry the largest premiums. Family-oriented amenities (children's books and toys, a changing table) also show premiums of about 20%, but these partly reflect larger homes, so they suggest who the listing is aimed at rather than something worth adding on its own. **Decision:** set a starting price from the market and room-type median, adjust for capacity, then add a premium for amenities that are hard to copy. The **Value picks** table flags well-reviewed listings priced 10% to 50% below comparable ones, which are candidates for a price increase.

**3. Demand over time: when to staff and when to raise prices.** Review volume, used as a proxy for stays, grew roughly 2.3x between 2016 and 2018 across these markets. The **Seasonality clock**, a radial chart with one bar per month, shows that peaks differ by hemisphere and climate. Montreal is strongly concentrated in July to September (August alone brings 15% of the year's reviews), Barcelona and Porto are busy from April to October, Rio peaks in January and February (the southern summer and Carnival) with a second bump in August, and the Hawaiian islands stay fairly flat all year. **Decision:** schedule cleaning staff and seasonal pricing per market instead of using one company-wide calendar.

**4. Host quality: what earns ratings.** The **Superhost effect** card pairs a dark ring (superhosts) with a light ring (regular hosts) for each measure. Superhosts average a 96.8 rating against 91.8 for regular hosts. They charge a median of 19% more, collect three times as many reviews per listing, and 94% of their listings are active against 55%. Ratings also fall as a host's portfolio grows: single-listing hosts average 94.4, while hosts with more than 10 listings average 90.7. That's a direct warning for a management company, whose whole model is a large portfolio. **Decision:** make superhost-level standards (fast responses, cleanliness, accurate listings) a company KPI, and use the **Where quality slips** card to see which sub-scores need attention. Cleanliness and value are the weakest sub-scores, especially for private rooms.

**5. Map: operational drill-down.** A 3D hexagon map extrudes listing density street by street for any chosen market, so an analyst can see where supply is crowded (competition) and where it's thin (gaps worth filling).

## Limitations

The data is a 2019 snapshot, so it shows market structure rather than current prices. Reviews are a proxy for demand, because only some guests leave one. "Days unavailable" mixes real bookings with dates the host blocked. Exchange rates are fixed approximations for February 2019. In a production version, the dashboard would read a regularly refreshed scrape and real booking data, but the same database design and dashboard structure would carry over.
